# Agriculture & Agribusiness — Uganda Context

## Market Overview
- Agriculture employs ~70% of Uganda's workforce and contributes ~24% of GDP
- Key crops: maize, beans, groundnuts, cassava, sesame (simsim), sorghum, rice, vegetables
- Northern Uganda (Palabek/Adjumani area): primarily maize, beans, groundnuts, sesame, vegetables
- Growing season: bimodal rainfall — March-May (first season) and August-November (second season)
- Off-season vegetable farming (irrigated) can command 2-3x premium prices

## Benchmarks for Small-Scale Agribusiness
- Gross margins: 30-50% for raw produce, 50-70% for processed/value-added products
- Common value addition: drying (groundnuts, maize), milling (flour), sorting/grading, packaging
- Breakeven for small-scale farming: typically 2-3 growing seasons
- Labor cost: UGX 5,000-10,000/day for casual farm workers (~$1.30-2.60)
- Transport to nearest trading center: UGX 500-2,000 per 100kg bag by boda-boda

## Distribution Channels
- Local markets (weekly market days in trading centers)
- Roadside sales to passing traders/trucks
- Aggregation points and cooperatives
- Direct supply to schools, restaurants, refugee settlement organizations (WFP, UNHCR suppliers)
- WhatsApp-based ordering for regular customers
- Cross-border trade to South Sudan and DRC (for settlements near borders)

## Common Challenges
- Post-harvest losses: 20-30% for cereals, 40%+ for perishables due to lack of storage
- Price volatility: harvest season glut drives prices down 40-60%
- Limited access to improved seeds and inputs
- Land tenure insecurity (especially for refugee farmers on allocated plots)
- Distance to markets and poor road conditions during rainy season

## Opportunities
- Storage and buy-low-sell-high strategies (hermetic bags cost ~$2 and reduce losses by 90%)
- Collective marketing through farmer groups for better bargaining power
- Value addition (milling, drying, packaging) to capture more margin
- Contract farming with organizations operating in refugee settlements
- Urban markets in Gulu, Lira, and Kampala willing to pay premiums for quality produce
